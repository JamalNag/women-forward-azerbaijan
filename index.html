A regional women's economic empowerment initiative · Azerbaijan · 2024–2026

Women Forward Azerbaijan

A two-year programme that helped women in smaller cities and rural communities turn skills they already had into income they could plan around. It combined field research, four learning pathways, hands-on modules built around each woman's own work, and twelve weeks of follow-up support.

The problem

Skills were there. The path to income was not.

Many women in Azerbaijan's regions have real professional, craft, agricultural and service skills. What they often lack are the practical tools to turn those skills into steady income.

Barriers named by participants

  • Little knowledge of cost calculation and pricing
  • Limited access to digital tools
  • Weak connections to markets and customers
  • No structured financial records
  • Low confidence when presenting their work

The aim was to build and test a regional model rather than run one more general entrepreneurship course. Participants were treated as partners: their experience, constraints and feedback shaped how the programme changed over time.

Needs assessment

We listened before we designed.

Before writing a single module, the team ran a structured needs assessment across all five regions.

The research found three different starting points, not one group:

  • Strong practical skills, but had never sold a product
  • Occasional customers, but no way to calculate costs or record income
  • Small-scale activity already running, but without the tools or connections to grow it

Key finding

Most women priced their work by the cost of materials alone. Their own working time, transport, packaging and customers' repeated revisions were left out. The team redesigned the training plan and added a dedicated module on the value of labour and full-cost pricing.

Methodology

Six stages, from first assessment to local ownership

Select a stage to see what it involved.

Each participant was assessed in eight areas. The results set her starting point and decided her learning pathway.

  • A marketable skill, product or service
  • Understanding of costs
  • Setting and explaining a price
  • Income and expense records
  • Communicating with customers
  • Presenting a product or service
  • Using digital sales channels
  • Confidence in independent economic decisions

Participants were placed on one of four pathways, so women with very different experience did not sit through the same programme.

See the four pathways below.

36 practical modules in six areas. Every module used the participant's own product or service and ended with something she could use straight away: a cost sheet, a product description, a catalogue, a financial record, a customer script or an action plan.

  • Product and service development
  • Cost calculation and pricing
  • Financial record-keeping
  • Digital presentation and catalogues
  • Customer communication and negotiation
  • Safe use of digital sales channels

Formal training was followed by twelve weeks of guided practice. Progress was reviewed every two weeks against concrete actions rather than attendance: a price calculated, a catalogue created, a customer approached, a first order, a repeat sale.

Participants learned to describe and present their work, then showed it at regional events, on professional pages, in individual digital catalogues and in a shared project catalogue.

The programme did not promise commercial success or present entrepreneurship as the answer for everyone. It gave women the means to test an opportunity and decide whether it suited their circumstances.

30 regional coordinators and community mentors were trained and received a complete implementation package. Afterwards they ran 24 practical sessions in 11 communities without the youth project leads present.

Outcomes

Measured by what changed, not by how many events were held.

Participants took the same assessment at the start and at the end. Monitoring tracked behaviour: calculating costs, keeping records, presenting work, receiving orders and making repeat sales.

What we learned

Incomplete cases were part of the evaluation.

The team studied why some women stopped or could not use certain tools, and used those cases to change the programme.

Barriers we documented

  • Limited internet access
  • Family and caregiving responsibilities
  • Transport difficulties
  • Too little time to take part
  • Differences in literacy and digital experience
  • Uneven access to local markets

Nine documented revisions

  • Long theoretical sessions replaced with shorter applied workshops
  • Written instructions supplemented with visual and voice-based materials
  • One common curriculum split into four pathways
  • Peer support added for women with limited digital experience
  • Repeat sales added as an indicator of lasting market activity
  • A dedicated module on safe customer communication
  • Cost-calculation exercises using participants' own products
  • An anonymous feedback channel
  • A community-mentor pathway for experienced participants

The programme did not treat entrepreneurship as a universal solution. It gave women better information for their own decisions.

Youth project leads

Two co-leads, two halves of the work

JN

Jamal Naghizade

Youth Co-Lead for Strategy, Monitoring and Impact Analysis

Helped design the baseline and final assessments, defined measurable indicators and built the monitoring system used to compare progress across pathways. He insisted on separating activity from impact: success meant observable changes in participants' behaviour, not counts of events. He consolidated data from the regional teams and helped build the replication package for 30 coordinators.
